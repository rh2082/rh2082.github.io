<h3>Introduction</h3>
This is a summary of a talk I gave recently at the University of Bath, discussing my career path and experience of the PhD application process!
I'm only an expert in my experience of the application process. I want to share what I learned because I went in knowing nothing, and succeeded - so I clearly did something right, and hope that what I learned can help others.

One of the key things I found unclear as a first generation student initially, was the difference between a PhD offer and a funding offer: a PhD offer and PhD funding are distinct.
There are essentially four routes to PhD funding: a UKRI studentship, a scholarship, supervisor funding or self-funded. Alternatively, you can pursue a Centre for Doctoral Training PhD, or a Doctoral Training Program.
I'm quite definitely not an expert on PhD funding routes - I mainly wanted to make clear the difference between a funded and non-funded PhD. I would encourage doing your own research to understand more, especially if you're interested in a CDT or DTP.

When it comes to applying for a PhD, the ultimate goal of the PhD application process, from the perspective of the institution, is to determine whether the applicant is capable of completing a PhD at that institution. 
There are a range of factors that go into assessing this, but often include grades, research experience, passion, fit with department, soft skills, and how you perform throughout the application process (personal statement and interview). 
I think a really key aspect of this goal is that it's whether you're capable of completing a PhD **in that place**, not necessarily whether you're capable of completing a PhD at all.
A rejection does not mean you are not capable of doing a PhD at all, it might just mean you didn’t fit that research group at that time. 

<h3>Grades/Degrees</h3>
There are two aspects here: which degrees you do and what you get in them. I found them so interlinked, it was hard to write two separate sections, so this part will be long.

Typically, STFC funded PhDs in physics require “a 2.1 or higher in BSc Physics or a related discipline.”
I have seen an ESPRC funded PhD which required a 2.2, so other options are certainly available, but a 2.1 seems to be typical. There may be variation for other funding sources. 
Note that a master’s degree, even an integrated one, is not required. That is not to say it wouldn't help, but there are other factors to consider.

Firstly, if you do an integrated masters and want to apply to PhDs while studying it, you may end up in a position where you're attending PhD interviews without having completed a full research project, depending on your degree structure.
It's very common that in the interview, you will be asked to give a presentation on a research project you have completed - I did this at all of my interviews.
Therefore, I think it is fair to say that applying before completing an integrated masters can be a disadvantage - universities do generally want to see some research skills/experience, and this can be difficult (though not impossible) to demonstrate without a completed final year project. 
It may well not be an issue: do not make it your reason not to apply, but I think it's worth knowing going in. Also, of course, a final year project is not the only way to obtain a completed research project. 

If you’re not on track to achieve a 2.1 in your undergraduate degree - BSc or masters - it’s worth considering further study. 
Regardless of what the university think of you, these entry requirements are attached to the funding body. One of the ways you can do this is through a postgraduate masters degree.

One of the major disadvantages of a postgraduate masters degree is the cost. Not only are postgraduate degrees not subject to the same tuition fee cap as undergraduate courses, but you will not get a maintenance loan.
The postgraduate loan is generally around £12000 (it goes up a bit each year), which is enough to cover the tuition fees of many masters courses. However, it will probably only leave you with a couple of hundred to live on.
It may leave you with more if you're able to access a graduate discount by studying your postgrad at the same university you did your undergraduate degree at.
Nonetheless, finances can be a restriction. This is part of a wider problem in academia and not one I'm going into extensive discussion on, but grr.

My choice to study a postgraduate masters was not influenced by my grades: it was influenced by my research interests. While I enjoyed my time at Bath, the opportunities for solar physics were limited.
In addition, many solar physics research groups are in the department of mathematics, rather than physics. Because of that, I wanted to get a more mathematical qualification, in case I applied for a PhD in a maths department.
Finally, I felt I didn't have enough research experience when I was finishing up my BSc. I essentially only had my final year project, as the side project I was doing was tied to the masters degree (so I would not have had it without that offer). 
I hadn't done any research internships because they all rejected me (and yet I got a PhD offer - rejection is redirection), so that made the masters feel critical.

It's also worth pointing out that masters by research exist, and their tuition fees are typically half that of taught masters. Unfortunately, there's so much choice and so many factors, that it's hard to capture it all clearly!

To conclude, some universities care about grades far more than others, but generally meeting the entry requirements is advised. 
It’s worth asking around to get a sense of how important the grades are to the department.

<h3>Research Experience</h3>
This is important for showing you understand what a PhD entails and that you’re capable of finishing one. It’s nice to have research experience outside of your degree, but it’s not a necessity. 
A good way to get research experience outside of your degree is through research internships, and I do have another page on this site that lists a huge range of internships available to undergraduate students. 
Alternatively, you can try emailing scientists who do research you’re interested in about working with them – but it’s unlikely you’ll be paid if you take this approach, so do keep this in mind.
Nonetheless, I will always be an advocate for emailing scientists whose work you're interested in. If they say yes, it could have a huge benefit - and the worst that happens is they say no.

I know people who have gotten PhD offers with:
*	BSc + research internship
*	MPhys only
*	BSc + MSc + random research project

I suspect it is also possible to get a PhD offer with just a BSc project depending on the field - my sample size is not massive.

It is important to note that a PhD is a learning course, not a research course. Research experience is useful, and you will have some if you have done a degree, so I wouldn't encourage being immensely stressed about this.
Additionally, I’ve seen advice against saying you want to do a PhD because you’re passionate about research, because the primary goal for you as an individual is to **learn** about your field. Learning and passion are arguably more key.

<h3>Passion</h3>
There are two aspects to this: the what and the why. 
You need to demonstrate that you are passionate about the topic you will be studying, and that you know why you want to study it – and why you want to pursue a PhD at all! 
There are a number of ways to demonstrate a passion for your field. Knowing it well is an option, but it is not the only option, and it is not required. 
For example, my experience really varied. At some of my interviews, there was no expectation for me to know anything about solar physics, and potential supervisors explained their research to me (things I did know already), because I was not expected to know. 
However, at some other interviews, I was questioned (grilled) about the field itself and knowledge was expected. 
It definitely varies across institutions, so I would advise speaking to students doing PhDs where you’re applying if you want department-specific advice on this.

As I said, knowledge is not the only way to communicate an interest in your field. You need to know enough to know why studying it is important to you, but you do not need an encyclopedic knowledge at all.
Arguably, demonstrating an ability and passion for learning are more important than an interest in your specific field. 
This can be done through the aforementioned research experience (whether in the correct field or not); through outreach and public engagement you've done; teaching; seminar attendance.
Your hobbies can also be a way of demonstrating a passion for learning - I spoke in one of my interviews about how I'd been learning British Sign Language, and I think that was well-received.

Ultimately, while you don’t need to know your subject itself, you absolutely do need to know why you want to study it! 
Why are you doing a PhD rather than getting a normal, well-paid job? Why is this field interesting and important to you?

<h3>Fit with Department</h3>
I don’t have lots to say about this, because it’s not really under your control. You have the personality you have!
Therefore, advice I’d give here is to be yourself in your interview. 
Don’t be unprofessional, but show your personality and your hobbies. 
It’s likely important to the institution that you’re going to fit into/engage with the pre-existing community. 
I would say that this is somewhere where volunteering work can be really valuable, as that's likely to be evidence of you engaging with a local community.

<h3>Soft Skills</h3>
The importance of these is, again, something that varies a lot by department. However, things to consider demonstrating are:
*	Can you manage your time?
*	Can you manage your project?
*	Can you communicate clearly, written and spoken?
*	Could you help organise a conference or a workshop?
You do not have to have every skill going in - after all, a PhD is a learning course! Nonetheless, it's nice to go in with an idea of your strengths and weaknesses - it's a reasonably common interview question.

<h3>The Application</h3>
Wow, you've made it through my long rambling advice about the qualities you are likely to need as a prospective PhD student. Now for some notes on the application process itself.
I think that every PhD I applied to requested a CV and a personal statement for the application itself. 
First and foremost, an academic CV is different to an industry CV. Education, publications (if you have them, I didn't), and skills come first. 
I don't want to give loads of advice on this because I'm not confident of my knowledge, but do some research about how best to structure a CV for a PhD application!
Secondly, the personal statement. This kind of follows a similar list to the qualities I've discussed already. Mine opened with why I wanted to study solar physics, and my relevant research experience.
I then tied this into my degrees and the choices I had made about them and the modules I studied, and how they would make me good at doing a PhD in solar physics.
I wrote about how I'd engaged with solar physics in an extra-curricular manner, through seminar attendance, for example. That led into the outreach talks I had given.
I wrote about my soft skills - mostly developed through volunteering. I focussed on things like time management and organisation because I knew these to be my strengths - I juggled a lot during my final year of undergrad!
Finally, I wrote specifically about the institution I was applying to. I described my interest in their research specifically, and how it linked to my goals and interests.
How much you tailor the personal statement to the insitution is up to you. I probably did overdo it, but it won't do any harm!

<h3>Interviews</h3>
Congratulations, you've made it all the way through this long post, and received an interview invite. 
In my opinion, the interviews are, at their core, an opportunity to talk about something you’re passionate about with other people who are also passionate about that thing! 
They do not need to be scary, even though they definitely are. Nonetheless, try not to be scared, because it’s an opportunity to meet lots of cool people and have lots of interesting conversations. 
You've been invited to interview because you earned it: you deserve to be there. Have faith in yourself.

You'll likely be asked to give a presentation on some research project you have completed. If you're at university, seek the advice of an academic on this.
The help I got from the academics at my university was invaluable. Definitely practice your talk - keeping to time will be essential. However, don't practice it to the point where it sounds like you're reciting it when you give it.
Something that really helped me was having the exact way I was going to say the title and supervisor of the project I was speaking about established in my mind.
It's a bit silly, but it meant I absolutely knew the first thing I was going to say, and it made it easier for everything else to follow.

Your presentation will likely be followed by some questions on that project. If you can’t answer every question, don’t panic – I could not answer every question in my interview. 
I would advise against making something up, it is better to admit that you don’t know. In a similar vein, don’t lie in your interview in general!
Definitely don’t downplay your achievements – you’ve got here because you deserve to be – but don’t tell outright lies.


The questions will then transition at some point into more general questions. Things like why you want to do a PhD, why you're interested in the topic...
