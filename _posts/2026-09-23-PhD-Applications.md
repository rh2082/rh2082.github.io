title: PhD Application Advice
---
Welcome to my PhD application preparation guide! This is all based on my experiences so has a sample size of one, but hopefully it gives some useful insights.

This PhD application guide is primarily geared towards preparing to apply for a PhD. It therefore focusses on some of the key things you might want to think about to become a good candidate for a PhD, rather than on the application process itself.
Firstly, why do you want to do a PhD instead of getting a normal, well-paid job and all the benefits thereof? Secondly, why do you want to do your PhD in this topic; what makes this field interesting and important to you? Understanding your answers to these questions is one of, if not the most important part of applying to a PhD!

When applying for a PhD, there are two things you are typically applying for: the PhD position itself, and the funding. There are essentially four routes to PhD funding: a UKRI studentship, a scholarship, supervisor funding or self-funded. In physics, the PhD position and funding typically come together – such as a UKRI studentship associated with a specific project. You can also pursue a PhD through the Centre for Doctoral Training (CDT), which has more ties to industry, but I have no experience of this and instead encourage you to do your own research.

Funding bodies will often have entry requirements associated with the studentship. For example, STFC funded PhDs typically require “a 2.1 or higher in BSc Physics or a related discipline.” Notably, a masters degree is not required. If you don’t meet this requirement, getting an offer will be a struggle. If you do, most universities will not look too far beyond this – but some will. Having a first-class degree is only going to help you, but a 2.1 should not hinder you significantly. I have also seen some funded projects which required a 2.2 or higher. 

If your undergraduate grades are below the entry requirements, a postgraduate masters degree will override this. Therefore, while expensive, this can be a good option if your grades aren’t what you need. A postgraduate masters can also be a great option if the field of research you’re interested in is not perfectly aligned with your undergraduate degree. For example, while my undergraduate degree was in physics, solar physics research groups tend to sit within any of the physics, maths or engineering departments. I decided to pursue an MSc in Mathematical and Theoretical Physics, which functioned as a joint honours course across the school of maths and school of physics. This gave me the mathematical backing and confidence I needed to apply to PhDs in maths departments – even though I ultimately ended up in a physics department.

While choosing your research project may feel like the most important aspect, choosing the supervisor is far, far more important. I, like many, would always advise choosing a better supervisor and a worse project over a worse supervisor and a better project. You will be working very closely with your supervisor for at least three years – you need to like them! They can support you and build you into an independent, confident, capable researcher, or, they can tear you down and make your PhD experience extremely unpleasant. With that being said, actually deciding who to work with is mostly based on vibes. You should speak to your potential supervisors live (whether online or in person) to get a feel for what they’re like. The gut feeling you come away from that interaction with can be enough.

Research experience is, predictably, a key thing universities will look for when you are applying for PhDs. Having research experience demonstrates your ability to cope with the intensity of doing research full time, and the associated challenges. Most interviews will ask you to present on a completed research project, so it is hugely advantageous if you have a large scale, completed research project when you are attending interviews. This can be difficult if you are doing an integrated masters degree, as you often do not complete your dissertation until after PhD interviews typically occur. It is therefore worth thinking about timing your application accordingly. However, this is not to say that your research experience has to go beyond your degree!* While it can feel like it, having publications before starting a PhD is not required, expected and certainly should not be the norm. At its core, a PhD is a training program where you are learning how to become an independent researcher – there’s no reason why you should need to know how to do that (via a publication) before starting. 

Being passionate about research is not the most important trait. A PhD is a learning program more than it is a research program, so being passionate about learning is the most important thing. There are a number of ways you can demonstrate this. The most obvious of these is having knowledge of your field because you’ve read around and attended seminars etc. However, having extensive knowledge of your PhD topic is not (and should not be!) a requirement. As a fair warning, there are some universities that will grill you on your research topic during interview, but prior knowledge is generally not required. Another way to demonstrate passion for learning is to highlight skills you’ve learned recently. In my PhD interview, I talked about how I had been learning British Sign Language and quadball alongside my degree. 

Soft skills are something departments are likely to look for when choosing PhD students. Things like time management, project management, organisation, communication – even managing money. You do not have to have every skill going in - after all, a PhD is a learning course! Nonetheless, it's nice to go in with an idea of your strengths and weaknesses - it's a reasonably common interview question. 

The final thing and, in some ways the most important, is how you fit into the department. When it comes to applying for a PhD, the ultimate goal of the PhD application process, from the perspective of the institution, is to determine whether the applicant is capable of completing a PhD at that institution, at that time. Do your research interests and goals align with those of the group? Will you fit into the group socially or are you likely to end up feeling isolated? Community is so critical to your PhD so while it is unlikely to be the most important factor, it is something the department – and you – should be considering. Even if you do get an offer, if you’re unsure about how you’ll fit into the group, ask for another visit. Find out about what life is like to work in that place. Consider this, and be sure to show some personality during your interview. 

While I said the focus of this post would not be on the application process itself, I would like to strongly recommend <a href="https://youtu.be/gxjFeKocLX4?si=u8gDAE-9OCAszlMb">this YouTube video</a> which discusses some of the most common interview questions from the perspective of an admissions tutor. In my opinion, the interviews are, at their core, an opportunity to talk about something you’re passionate about with other people who are also passionate about that thing! They do not need to be scary, even though they definitely are. Nonetheless, try not to be scared, because it’s an opportunity to meet lots of cool people and have lots of interesting conversations. You’ve been invited to interview because you earned it: you deserve to be there. Have faith in yourself.
I wish you the best of luck with your applications!

*If you are keen to acquire research experience beyond your degree, see my other post detailing research internships for undergraduates. Alternatively, email people whose research you are interested in! The worst they can say is no!
